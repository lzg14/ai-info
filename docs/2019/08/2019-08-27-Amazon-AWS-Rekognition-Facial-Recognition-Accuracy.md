title: Amazon AWS Rekognition facial recognition faces scrutiny as accuracy concerns mount
date: 2019-08-27
source: ACLU
url: https://www.aclu.org/blog/technological-innovation/digital-and-privacy-technology-analysis/amazon-keepers-shelf
---

Amazon's facial recognition technology, AWS Rekognition, came under intensifying scrutiny as advocacy groups and researchers raised concerns about accuracy disparities and potential misuse by law enforcement agencies. The American Civil Liberties Union and other organizations called on Amazon to stop selling facial recognition technology to governments, citing risks to civil liberties and evidence that the system misidentified people of color at significantly higher rates than white faces.

A study conducted by MIT researcher Joy Buolamwini demonstrated that Amazon Rekognition had particular difficulty accurately identifying the gender of darker-skinned women, with error rates reaching nearly 31 percent compared to 0 percent for lighter-skinned males. These findings added to growing evidence that many AI systems exhibited problematic biases reflecting the demographics of their training data. The study joins similar findings about other commercial facial recognition systems, suggesting systemic issues across the industry.

Amazon disputed the research methodology and conclusions, arguing that newer versions of the service had improved accuracy and that facial recognition should be evaluated for specific use cases rather than general capability. The company maintained that the technology could be valuable for legitimate purposes including identifying missing children and preventing human trafficking. Amazon published guidelines for responsible use of Rekognition and recommended that law enforcement agencies implement additional human oversight when using facial recognition for investigative purposes.

The controversy prompted discussions in Congress about potential regulation of facial recognition technology. Legislators from both parties expressed concerns about privacy implications and the potential for abuse, though proposals for specific restrictions made limited progress. Several cities including San Francisco and Oakland banned government use of facial recognition, reflecting local concerns about surveillance and civil liberties.

Amazon shareholders and employees joined the debate, with some investors proposing resolutions calling for greater oversight and transparency about facial recognition sales. The growing opposition illustrated the challenges technology companies faced as they developed powerful AI capabilities that could be applied in ways that conflicted with user privacy or civil liberties. The industry-wide pattern of accuracy disparities suggested that addressing bias would require fundamental changes in how AI systems were developed and tested.
