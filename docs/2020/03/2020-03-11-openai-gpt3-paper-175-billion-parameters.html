OpenAI Publishes GPT-3 Paper: 175 Billion Parameters and Few-Shot Learning Breakthrough

arXiv / OpenAI2020-03-11入库 2020-03-11

In March 2020, OpenAI published the landmark paper "Language Models are Few-Shot Learners" introducing GPT-3, a 175 billion parameter autoregressive language model that represented a quantum leap in natural language processing capabilities. This academic publication preceded the API launch later that year and provided the first comprehensive look at the model's architecture, training process, and remarkable emergent abilities.

GPT-3's scale was unprecedented in the AI field, with 96 attention layers and 3,752 trainable parameters per parameter, trained on approximately 410 billion tokens from Common Crawl, WebText, Wikipedia, and other curated datasets. The model's training compute cost was estimated at 3.6 × 10^23 FLOPs, requiring significant computational resources and engineering innovation.

The paper's most striking findings concerned few-shot learning capabilities—the ability to perform new tasks with minimal examples provided in the input prompt, without gradient updates. GPT-3 demonstrated strong performance across diverse benchmarks including translation, question answering, cloze tasks, and news article writing, often matching or exceeding state-of-the-art fine-tuned models.

OpenAI explored various model sizes in the GPT-3 paper, ranging from 125 million to 175 billion parameters, showing smooth scaling laws where capability improvements correlated predictably with model size and training compute. This systematic approach to understanding scaling behavior provided valuable guidance for future large model development.

The publication generated intense academic and industry interest, with researchers studying GPT-3's abilities and limitations including occasional biases inherited from training data, struggles with rare words, and tendency to generate plausible but incorrect information. The work fundamentally shifted expectations about what transformer language models could achieve and catalyzed widespread research into prompt-based learning paradigms.
