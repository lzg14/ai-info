Google Releases Speech Recognition Model Trained on 10 Billion Hours of Data

机器之心2020-12-20入库 2020-12-20

Google researchers announced a significant breakthrough in automatic speech recognition in December 2020, revealing that they had developed a speech recognition model trained on approximately 10 billion hours of audio data. This massive scale of training data represented an unprecedented approach to building robust speech recognition systems, enabling the model to achieve state-of-the-art performance across a wide range of languages, dialects, and acoustic conditions.

The announcement built upon earlier work from Google's Brain team, which had demonstrated that neural network-based models trained on large amounts of unlabeled audio data could learn powerful representations useful for speech recognition. The 10-billion-hour threshold was particularly notable because it represented a roughly ten-fold increase over previously reported training datasets for speech recognition systems.

The technical approach combined self-supervised learning techniques with traditional supervised fine-tuning. By pre-training on massive amounts of unlabeled speech audio, the model learned rich acoustic representations that could then be adapted to specific recognition tasks with much smaller amounts of labeled data. This approach addressed one of the persistent challenges in speech recognition: the high cost of acquiring human-labeled training data.

Performance evaluations demonstrated remarkable improvements in challenging scenarios, including recognition of accented speech, transcription in noisy environments, and handling of code-switching between languages. The model showed particular improvements on speech recognition for languages with limited training data, a persistent challenge in building inclusive speech technology.

Google indicated that this technology would be rolled out across various Google products, improving the accuracy of voice typing, voice search, and real-time translation features. The research team published their findings, contributing to the broader ML community's understanding of scaling laws for speech recognition.
